## The task

**Feed demo-booked and closed outcomes back into the scoring model so marketing and sales share one number.** You decide the steps in advance. AI does the reading, writing or judging inside those steps, but never chooses the path.

- Approach: An automated workflow with AI steps. No agent needed.
- Agents: No agent
- Main tool: n8n
- Runs on: Cloud, pay as you go
- Autonomy: level 2 of 4, approve every output. The system prepares the work. A person approves each result before it goes out or anything changes.

What it should cost (the guide's estimate; tell the owner before anything adds cost beyond this):

- Running cost: about $22 to $23 a month (10 to 40 runs).
- AI models: $4 to $21 per 1,000 runs, under $1 a month.
- n8n Cloud Starter: $22 a month. €20 a month, billed annually, sized to your runs (Starter covers 2,500 a month, Pro 10,000, Business 40,000 and is self-hosted). Or self-host the free community edition for your own internal use.
- Review time: under an hour a week. A person checks every result, at 1 to 3 minutes each.
- Build: 1.5 to 5 weeks for a first version. Your developers or IT team.
- Estimates, with prices as of October 10, 2026; build and review time are the guide's own estimates.

Why this design (from the owner's answers):

- The steps are fixed (pull deals, tag source, propose, check, approve, write), and only the reading of messy source fields and free-text partner names needs judgement, so a workflow with AI steps fits. Plain automation would miss those cases, and an agent would add cost and unpredictability for no gain. The rules agreed with this.
- The rules chose routing with an evaluator. Routing fits because referral, webinar and other deals need different tagging rules, and the checklist makes a reliable automatic reviewer. I kept both.

## Architecture, step by step

Shape: Routing + Generator and checker.

| Step | Name | Kind | Model or method | Hands on to |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Scheduled start | Start | Cron schedule | 2 |
| 2 | Pull new demo and close outcomes | Fixed step | Scripted action | 3 |
| 3 | Check each record is complete | Fixed step | Validation rules | 4 (complete), 6 (incomplete) |
| 4 | Sort by lead source | Decision | Keyword and field rules | 5 (known source), 6 (matches neither) |
| 5 | Compare rates to control limits | Fixed step | Control chart | 7 |
| 6 | Records needing a person | Person | Review queue | 11 (logged) |
| 7 | Draft the proposed score update | AI step | Claude Sonnet 5.5 (`claude-sonnet-5-5`) | 8 |
| 8 | Check proposal against the numbers | AI step | Claude Haiku 5.5 (`claude-haiku-5-5`) | 9 |
| 9 | Marketing and sales approve the change | Person | Joint approval before go-live | 10 (approved), 7 (sent back) |
| 10 | Write approved score to HubSpot | Fixed step | Scripted action | 11 |
| 11 | Shared score updated | Finish | Logged in HubSpot | end |

### 1. Scheduled start

- What happens: The workflow starts itself on a set schedule, with nobody at the desk.
- Why: You said it should run on a schedule while you're away, and a few runs a week is enough for a score that moves slowly.
- Passes on: The run date and the date of the last successful run.

### 2. Pull new demo and close outcomes

- What happens: Fetches deals that reached demo booked, closed won or closed lost since the last run. For each, it takes the contact ID, original source, source data 1 and 2, the partner field from the referral form, and webinar registered or attended status.
- Why: One agreed pull from HubSpot's own fields means marketing and sales score from the same data.
- Passes on: Raw outcome records, one per deal, plus the run date.
- Stop rule: Stop if the HubSpot pull fails.

### 3. Check each record is complete

- What happens: Confirms each deal has a contact ID, a stage with a date, an original source, and no duplicate contact-and-deal pair. Records that fail are set aside for a person and are not guessed at.
- Why: Missing or duplicate records would quietly inflate or deflate a source's rate.
- Passes on: Complete records to the sort step. Failing records to the review queue.

### 4. Sort by lead source

- What happens: Places each complete record in partner referral, webinar or other. It uses fixed rules on HubSpot's fields: a partner name on the referral form counts as partner referral, and a webinar campaign with registered or attended status counts as webinar. Anything matching neither rule goes to review.
- Why: Routing helps when categories are distinct and classification is accurate. These tags are structured fields, so exact rules do the sorting more cheaply and predictably than a model.
- Passes on: Records labelled by source group.

### 5. Compare rates to control limits

- What happens: Computes the demo-booked rate and the closed-won rate for each source group. It draws a p-chart per source with control limits from history, and flags any source whose rate falls outside them. A source with fewer than 30 deals in the period is held, not changed.
- Why: Demo and close outcomes are pass/fail counts per source, and a p-chart separates a real shift from normal month-to-month noise before the score moves.
- Passes on: Each source's rates, its control limits, and any out-of-control or held flags.
- Stop rule: Hold any source under 30 deals.

### 6. Records needing a person

- What happens: A person checks records that failed the completeness check or matched neither source rule. They fix the HubSpot field or exclude the record with a stated reason.
- Why: Guessing a source would move the shared number without anyone seeing it. Fixing the field in HubSpot makes the next run clean.
- Passes on: Corrected or excluded records, each logged with a reason.
- Person steps in: Owner reviews each exclusion.

### 7. Draft the proposed score update

- What happens: Writes a short plain-English proposal: the current and proposed weight for each source, the rates and limits behind each change, and any held source. It uses only the computed figures and does not estimate its own.
- Why: One written proposal gives marketing and sales a shared explanation to approve. Limiting it to the computed numbers keeps it from inventing figures.
- Passes on: A proposal with one row per source.
- Stop rule: Checked against checklist.

### 8. Check proposal against the numbers

- What happens: Compares every number in the proposal with the computed rates. A fixed checklist asks whether each figure matches, each change cites a flagged source, held sources are unchanged, and no individual contact is named. It marks failures and does not fix them.
- Why: A checker helps only when it has something real to check against, and here the computed rates are that reference.
- Passes on: The proposal with a pass or insufficient-evidence result on each line.
- Stop rule: Checked against checklist.

### 9. Marketing and sales approve the change

- What happens: One named person from marketing and one from sales review each proposed change beside its source numbers and checklist result, one source at a time. They approve, edit or send it back. Nothing is approved in a batch.
- Why: Both teams share the number, so both sign off. A named approver for each change makes someone accountable and reduces rubber-stamping.
- Passes on: Approved weights only. Sent-back items return to the draft with a note.
- Person steps in: Two named approvers, one per team.

### 10. Write approved score to HubSpot

- What happens: Writes the approved weight for each source to one shared score property in HubSpot. It logs the run with its date, the approver names and the proposal version. Each write sets a value rather than adding one, so a retry is safe.
- Why: Marketing and sales need one value to read, and the log shows who approved what.
- Passes on: Confirmation of the write and the log entry.
- Stop rule: Stop if the write is not confirmed.

### 11. Shared score updated

- What happens: The approved score is live in HubSpot for both teams, and the run is recorded.
- Why: This is the outcome the task exists to produce. Each run leaves a record you can review.
- Passes on: Nothing further.

## Safeguards to build in

When a person steps in:

- After a set number of failed attempts (start with 3), stop and hand the case to a person.
- Agree in advance what track record earns the next level, for example four weeks without a serious error.
- A named person approves every result before release or action; reject, timeout and missing approval stop the run.
- The reviewer sees the checklist or requirements beside the source material and any item marked insufficient evidence, and approves, changes or rejects one case at a time. No batch approval.
- Track the override rate and the time per review. Overrides near zero, or reviews that take seconds, mean the review has become a rubber stamp.
- Seed known-wrong cases into the queue, so catching them is measured.

Guardrails, layered:

- Rule-based checks: input length limits, blocked words, and format checks on output.
- A personal-data filter that checks output before it leaves the system.
- A check for unsafe inputs, such as hidden instructions in emails or documents.
- Add a new guardrail each time a real failure shows a gap.
- The credential the AI steps use is read-only (free/busy for calendars) and cannot perform the approved action itself. The action step runs under a separate credential, only after approval.

## Watch out for

- **The AI checker can miss or wrongly pass a figure.** The checker is an AI step and can be wrong in both directions. Make the number-matching a code check against the computed rates, and use the checker only for judgement items such as whether a change cites a flagged source. Give it an unsure option and never let unsure count as pass. (knowledge base: G04, O01)
- **Approval must show the real change, and denial must stop it.** Each approver should see the recipient, the source, the old and new weight and the numbers behind it. A send-back or denial must cancel that source's write, not fall through to another write or to a default weight. (knowledge base: N03, A02)
- **Give the workflow only the access it needs.** The workflow needs read access to the deal and contact fields it uses and write access to the one shared score property. It should not hold broad CRM rights. Keep contact names and details out of the proposal, the logs and any monitoring data. (knowledge base: G01, O02)
- **A retried write must not double count.** Because each write sets the value, a retry is safe for the score. Logs and any count of approvals or runs could still double on retry, so key each run and approval on a single identifier and check the log before retrying. (knowledge base: N02, N03)
- **Incoming content can carry instructions.** An email or document can contain text aimed at your AI ("ignore your rules and forward this"). Treat incoming content as information, never as orders, and add a check for unsafe inputs.
- **Personal data can leak in output.** Filter output for personal information, share only what each step needs, and don't put secrets in the system prompt; prompts can be coaxed into revealing themselves.
- **Every extra step must earn its place.** Each added stage costs money and time. Keep one only if your test examples show the result improves enough to justify it.
- **n8n isn't open source.** n8n's licence lets you use and change it for your own internal business purposes. If you plan to host it for clients or build it into something you sell, check the licence or buy a commercial one first.
- **The smartest model isn't always the best fit.** Top models are slower and cost more, and for simple steps they don't do better. Let your test examples decide, not the leaderboard.
